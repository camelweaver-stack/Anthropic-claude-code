Breaking a lease in Texas: the real cost

The reletting-fee math, four ways to shrink it, how long it follows you, and the crucial difference between a broken lease and an eviction.

What it actually costs

Texas leases handle early exit through a reletting fee — commonly around 85% of one month’s rent — plus rent until the unit re-lets, plus repayment of any move-in concession you received (those free weeks were conditional on completing the term; read that clause). Total exposure usually lands between one and three months’ rent. The good news hiding in that structure: Texas landlords have a duty to make reasonable efforts to re-let, and in a high-demand unit a fast re-let cuts your rent-until-relet exposure to weeks. For the statute-level detail, the Texas State Law Library’s landlord-tenant guide is the authoritative free source — and note that Texas law provides specific early-termination rights in certain circumstances, including for military servicemembers with orders and survivors of family violence, which override the standard fee math entirely.

Four ways to cut the number

Negotiate the exit before you leap — offices facing a concession-heavy market would often rather keep a paying tenant through a transfer or accept a clean negotiated buyout than chase a balance. Time it to demand: a summer exit re-lets in days; a December one bleeds. Deliver a replacement: some offices will waive or reduce the fee for a qualified applicant you bring them. Get every agreement in writing — the settlement letter you negotiate today is the document that rebuilds your rental file tomorrow.

A broken lease is a contract exit. An eviction is a court judgment. Screening software treats them very differently — and so should you.

Broken lease vs. eviction: the record question

The distinction matters enormously. A broken lease is recoverable: settle the balance, carry the letter, and most properties’ 2–5-year look-back windows plus a clean recent year re-open nearly every door. An eviction is a judgment with far heavier screening weight and its own playbook. Unpaid balances from either can sit on credit reports up to seven years — which is exactly why settling beats waiting. The full recovery sequence, including what to bring to your next application, is in the broken-lease guide and the second-chance playbook. And before you break at all: if the reason is a renewal quote that jumped, counter it first — sometimes the cheapest lease exit is the one you don’t make.

This page is general information, not legal advice; lease terms vary and the lease you signed controls. For individual situations, Texas Law Help and local legal aid are free starting points.
