Utilities setup: who to actually call

The corridor's utility map has one twist that surprises every arrival — parts of it aren't in the 'choose your electric company' system at all. Here's the who-to-call file.

Electricity — check which system your address is in first

This is the corridor's big utilities twist. Much of the area sits in Oncor's wires territory, where Texas's deregulated market applies: you choose a retail provider at powertochoose.org (the state's official comparison site), and Oncor handles the poles, meters, and outages regardless of who bills you. But significant stretches of eastern Parker County — including parts of the Aledo and Willow Park area — are served by Tri-County Electric Cooperative, a member-owned co-op where there's no shopping: the co-op is the provider, with its own rates and a capital-credit structure. The address decides, not the city — so before comparing plans, confirm which territory the exact address is in (the previous occupant's bill, or a call to either, settles it). The cost side — average bills, plan-shopping strategy, the summer spike — lives in the utility cost guide.

The rest of the calls, in order

Gas: where a home has it, natural gas service in this region is Atmos Energy — though a real share of corridor homes, especially acreage and newer all-electric builds, have no gas at all, and the land pockets run propane (tank lease or purchase — the acreage checklist covers it). Water, sewer, trash: through your city's utility office (the city-services file); on acreage, it's well-and-septic territory with the trash contract often a private subscription. Internet: genuinely address-by-address out here — the availability guide is the check to run before lease or contract, not after. Timing: electric and water can usually start same-week; internet installs queue longest, so order it the day the contract firms up. The whole sequence, checkbox by checkbox, is phase five of the move checklist.
