Moving from New York to Fort Worth's west side: what a salary becomes

The steepest income-tax delta in the series — NYC's combined state-and-city stack reaches 14.776% — and the starkest lifestyle trade. Both deserve straight numbers.

The stacked-tax math

New York State runs nine brackets from 4% to 10.9% (the FY2026 budget trimmed the bottom five rates by 0.1 point starting this tax year, with the millionaire brackets extended into the 2030s), against a standard deduction of just $8,000 single / $16,050 joint. New York City then adds its own resident tax of 3.078% to 3.876% — with the top city rate starting at only $50,000 of taxable income. Stacked, a city household's combined marginal rate reaches 10.1% at ordinary professional incomes and up to 14.776% at the top — the heaviest state-and-local income tax in America. Concretely: a $100,000 single filer in the five boroughs pays roughly $7,900 a year in state-plus-city income tax; at $200,000 the figure clears $17,000. In Texas the entire stack is zero — the largest single-line saving on any page in this series.

The housing conversion

The second number is the rent-to-mortgage conversion. A $3,500–$4,500 city rent — ordinary for a family-sized NYC apartment — covers the entire monthly cost of a $425,000 Willow Park house: principal, interest, the ~$7,200 post-homestead property-tax line computed below, and insurance, with margin. (Run your own numbers in the affordability calculator — it prices the corridor's actual tax rates, not national averages.) New York's property taxes look gentle statewide (~1.3% average) but NYC's co-op/condo structures hide the burden in maintenance; suburban Westchester and Long Island bills routinely exceed anything this corridor produces. The move's fine print: Texas insurance is top-five expensive (hail), and New York State famously audits departing high earners — document the domicile change properly.

What you give up

The city. There's no suburban answer to New York — the transit, the depth of everything, the walk-out-your-door life. This corridor is its deliberate opposite: space, districts, drive-everywhere, new. The honest version of this move is choosing a different life, with the tax and housing math as the enabling arithmetic rather than the reason. For the household ready to make that choice, the sequence is in the relocation hub — rent one lease first (match quiz, current specials), then buy the district (the rent-vs-buy math on our observed data).

The Texas side of the ledger, computed honestly

Texas has no state income tax — and funds itself instead through property tax, which on this corridor runs from 1.902% of assessed value in Weatherford (Weatherford ISD) to 2.353% in the Fort Worth-in-Parker-County slice of Aledo ISD, with Willow Park at 2.091% and the city of Aledo at 2.056% (2024–2025 published rates; the full stack is on the corridor tax table). On a $425,000 house in Willow Park, the sticker math is about $8,890 a year — but Texas's homestead exemption now removes $140,000 of value from the school portion (raised by the November 2025 constitutional amendment), which cuts roughly $1,690 off that bill, landing near $7,200 a year before any additional city or county homestead percentages. The homestead cap then limits assessed-value growth to 10% a year while you live there. Two more honest lines: Texas home insurance is also among the country's most expensive (the corridor's hail belt is why), and sales tax here is 8.25%. The property-tax filing sequence that makes all of this real is in the buyer's property-tax playbook.
