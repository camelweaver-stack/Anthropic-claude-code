Moving from Colorado to Fort Worth's west side: the 4.4% question

Colorado is the closest call in this series — a modest flat tax, low property tax, and mountains. The math still moves, but it moves on housing and on what your income buys, not on a dramatic tax spread.

The tax math, stated fairly

Colorado taxes all income at a flat 4.4% (occasionally trimmed further in TABOR refund years), computed from federal taxable income — so a $100,000 single filer owes roughly $3,700–$4,400 a year depending on deductions. That's the smallest income-tax bill in this series, and Colorado pairs it with one of the nation's lowest property-tax burdens, around 0.5–0.6% effective. Texas inverts the structure: the income line goes to zero, and the property line rises to the corridor's 1.9–2.35%. On equal house prices Colorado wins the property side outright — the move pencils because the houses aren't equal. Denver-metro entry for a family house has run well above this corridor's $350,000–$500,000 new-construction band, and the income-tax savings (~$4,000–$6,600 a year for a $150K household) offsets most of the property-tax difference on a corridor-priced house.

The overlap nobody mentions: you already live in the hail belt

Colorado's Front Range and North Texas share the same insurance problem — hail — and Colorado premiums have lately risen faster than almost any state's. So the usual Texas-insurance warning lands softer here: you already budget for impact-resistant roofing and comprehensive glass. Sales tax is close to a wash (Denver ~8.8% vs 8.25% here). The daily-life delta concentrates in housing supply: the corridor's building pipeline means a relocating family chooses among competing new communities with tracked incentives instead of bidding against scarcity.

What you give up

The mountains. There is no Texan answer to a Tuesday-evening trailhead at 8,000 feet, and 300 days of low-humidity sunshine become 100°F Julys with real humidity swings. Skiing becomes a flight. What you get back: a housing ledger with slack in it, district-anchored family geography, and — for remote workers priced out of the Front Range — a corridor where fiber reaches most new addresses and the mortgage doesn't require two maxed incomes. Start with the match quiz if renting first, or the rent-vs-buy math if not.

The Texas side of the ledger, computed honestly

Texas has no state income tax — and funds itself instead through property tax, which on this corridor runs from 1.902% of assessed value in Weatherford (Weatherford ISD) to 2.353% in the Fort Worth-in-Parker-County slice of Aledo ISD, with Willow Park at 2.091% and the city of Aledo at 2.056% (2024–2025 published rates; the full stack is on the corridor tax table). On a $425,000 house in Willow Park, the sticker math is about $8,890 a year — but Texas's homestead exemption now removes $140,000 of value from the school portion (raised by the November 2025 constitutional amendment), which cuts roughly $1,690 off that bill, landing near $7,200 a year before any additional city or county homestead percentages. The homestead cap then limits assessed-value growth to 10% a year while you live there. Two more honest lines: Texas home insurance is also among the country's most expensive (the corridor's hail belt is why), and sales tax here is 8.25%. The property-tax filing sequence that makes all of this real is in the buyer's property-tax playbook.
